It's a loan, not free money

Deferred taxes plus accumulating interest are repaid when you sell or transfer the property, and the province places a restrictive lien on your title while deferred. This tool presents the trade-off; it doesn't tell you whether to defer.

How deferment actually works

The two BC deferment programs, what they require, and what they cost.

The two programs

Regular Program: for homeowners who are 55 or older, a surviving spouse of any age, or a person with disabilities. There's a $60 one-time application fee and a $10 annual renewal fee. Minimum equity required: 25% of your property's assessed value, and you have to maintain that equity while deferring.

Families with Children Program: for homeowners financially supporting a dependent child under 18, or an adult child with a disability, or one attending a post-secondary institution. No application or renewal fees. Minimum equity required: 15% of your property's assessed value, also maintained throughout the deferment.

Both programs also require at least one year of B.C. residency before applying, and both require that all previous years' property taxes, utility fees, penalties, and interest are already paid. Deferment only applies to the current year's taxes.

The interest, and why 2026 changed the math

Deferred taxes accrue interest until repaid. For tax years 2025 and earlier, that interest was simple and the two programs had different rates (Regular ran two points below prime; Families with Children ran at prime). Starting with the 2026 tax year, both programs charge compound interest at prime plus 2%, currently 6.45%, compounding monthly rather than once. In practice, that means interest is charged on interest that's already accrued, so a deferred balance grows faster over time than it would have under the old rules. Taxes deferred in 2025 or earlier keep the old simple-interest terms; only 2026-and-later deferrals are affected.

What happens when you sell or transfer

The full deferred amount, plus all interest that's accumulated, becomes due at that point. Until then, the province registers a restrictive lien against your property's title, which can come up if you try to refinance or take out a new line of credit while taxes are deferred.

Should you defer?

That depends on your own finances and plans, not something a generic tool can answer for you. The trade-off is real in both directions: deferring frees up cash today, but the bill you eventually owe grows every year it's outstanding, faster now than under the pre-2026 rules. If you're weighing it, the government's own program page and a look at your own long-term plans for the property are the right starting points.

Common deferment questions

Can I defer only part of my property tax bill? No. Deferment applies to your full current-year property tax amount, not a partial payment; you either defer the whole eligible balance for the year or pay it in full.

Does deferring my taxes affect my home insurance or mortgage? Deferment itself doesn't change your mortgage terms, but most mortgage agreements require property taxes to be paid, so check with your lender before deferring if your mortgage is still active; some lenders require consent.

Can I still sell my house if I have deferred taxes? Yes. You can sell at any time; the deferred amount plus accumulated interest is simply paid out of the sale proceeds at closing, similar to paying off a mortgage.

Do I have to reapply every year? Yes, both programs require an annual renewal application confirming you still meet the eligibility requirements (equity, residency, and no arrears), though the Families with Children Program renewal has no fee.

Is property tax deferment the same as a property tax exemption or rebate? No. A deferment is a loan you eventually repay with interest; it's different from the Home Owner Grant (a partial tax reduction most BC homeowners can claim regardless of deferment status) or any outright exemption.
